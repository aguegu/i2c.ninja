Over a decade, Weihong, who makes this site, played with a lot of I2C chips: motion sensors, humidity and temperature sensors, displays, EEPROMs, power monitors. Each time, the driver ended up written for one microcontroller, one board, one framework, and stayed there. What was wanted instead:

> I want to have a chip-independent drivers for all the i2c devices I played with over one decade. I would like the datasheet in code, not binding to specific chips.

A driver loyal to its datasheet, then, not to an architecture, a microcontroller family or an operating system. Its registers, its sequences and its limits written down once, as the datasheet gives them, and run anywhere something can carry bytes to the chip.

That is what [atiic](https://github.com/aguegu/atiic) is for, and i2c.ninja is the place where its drivers are seen working.

## The first try

Both are older than this site. i2c.ninja began in March 2022 as a web page showing what an I2C sensor reads, and atiic in April 2022 as its drivers. The browser couldn't talk I2C, so a small bridge stood in between, firmware of our own on a microcontroller. It worked, and by 2024 it had stopped moving.

On 4 November 2026 both came back, from an empty tree.

## atiic, adapter-free

The restart kept the idea and dropped everything that tied it down. The rule:

> so the design is simple. ch341t is just an adapter. And we try to make the device adapter free. We would add device driver one by one along the way because I would like it tested on real world.

A driver knows only a bus: write, read, write-then-read. Whatever provides those (a USB dongle today, something else tomorrow) is an adapter, and a driver never imports one. Nothing came back in bulk from the old code: each driver returns when it has run on a real board, with a test that can be run again. Which is where a datasheet in code pays off:

> device driver could be verified on linux with modern language and unit test suite. Without trying to make it work on specific chip by refreshing over and over.

Read the datasheet, write the driver, run it against the real chip from a laptop, in seconds, as many times as it takes. No firmware to flash between tries.

## i2c.ninja, for teaching

The site got the same restart ("which also need a 'reload'"): one public repository now, published on every push, built small (Vite, Vue, Tailwind CSS, native controls). And its purpose, said on the first day:

> The purpose of i2c.ninja is education. It visualizes i2c sensor data.

So each page does two jobs: it shows a driver working on a real chip, live, and it explains what the chip measures and how. How a browser reaches the chip at all is [the next story](/blog/a-dongle-for-a-bridge).

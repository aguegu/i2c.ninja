i2c.ninja is older than this site. It began in March 2022 as a web page that showed what an I2C sensor reads, and next to it grew [atiic](https://github.com/aguegu/atiic), a set of drivers for those sensors, from April 2022. The browser couldn't talk I2C, so a bridge stood in between: a small 8051-family microcontroller, an STC, taking commands over a serial line and turning them into I2C. It worked, and by 2024 it had stopped moving.

On 4 November 2026 both came back, from an empty tree. This post is about that restart: why, what changed, and the first days.

## The bridge that wasn't needed

The restart began with a question about a cheap USB dongle, the CH341T, which has an I2C mode:

> I know ch341t can work as a uart-i2c bridge, but I have never test that. I have a ch341t(i2c mode) attached to r8845, and its i2c end connected to a mpu6050. can you probe it?

It answered, and the MPU-6050 on the other end answered through it. That settled more than the probe:

> I have built a uart-i2c bridge on a stc 51 mcu before, which is deprecated long ago. If I would build it again, I would use wch chips. Since wch ch341t can do the bridge job. I may skip making our own bridge at all. and use ch341t instead.

And the reason it was worth it: a browser can open the CH341 itself, over WebUSB, with no bridge firmware and nothing to install.

> Since, this approach would work with webUSB. I think I would go this path. It is not about building a product on it. It is basically about testing i2c devices drivers. And web app like ble.i2c.ninja. (https://i2c.ninja) can visualize the readings from these sensors.

## atiic, adapter-free, one driver at a time

atiic went to 1.0.0 that afternoon and was rebuilt around one rule:

> so the design is simple. ch341t is just an adapter. And we try to make the device adapter free. We would add device driver one by one along the way because I would like it tested on real world.

A driver only speaks to a bus (write, read, write-then-read); the CH341 is one way to provide that bus, and another adapter can join later without touching a driver. Nothing was ported in bulk from the old version: each driver comes back when it has run on a real board, with a test that can be run again. Soon after came the point of it, in one sentence:

> device driver could be verified on linux with modern language and unit test suite. Without trying to make it work on specific chip by refreshing over and over.

## i2c.ninja, one repository, one purpose

The site needed the same restart:

> Let's move on to ble.i2c.ninja now. which also need a 'reload'.

There were two repositories, a private one with the source and a public one serving the built site. Now there is one, public, where every push builds and publishes the site. The stack is small on purpose: Vite, Vue, Tailwind CSS and native controls, no component library. And what it is for was said early, and has steered every page since:

> The purpose of i2c.ninja is education. It visualizes i2c sensor data.

The first device page was the [MPU-6050](/mpu6050), the chip that started it all on the bench. Then [Get started](/get-started), because a website user has no reason to know about Linux's udev rules or Windows' WinUSB driver, and both stand between a browser and the dongle. On Linux there is a third: the kernel's own `spi-ch341` driver claims the dongle on every plug, and the page shows how to let go of it. On Android it simply works: "I have tried i2c.ninja on android chrome with OTG, it works."

## Sensors, one at a time

After that the sensors came back as they were found and wired up, each only once its driver had passed on the board and its page on the bench: the [SHT20](/sht20) and [AHT21B](/aht21) for humidity, the [VL53L1X](/vl53l1x) for distance, the [INA231](/ina231) for power ("I believe it is a ina231, check the old atiic driver"), the [MAX30102](/max30102) for a heartbeat in a fingertip, the [MLX90614](/mlx90614) for temperature at a distance. At some point:

> Okay, I think i am running out of sensors. :)

Then three more turned up in a drawer, bought in 2012 for a first drone. That is [the next story](/blog/three-sensors-from-2012).

Along the way the site got the rest of what a public site needs: pages search engines can read, a light and dark theme, a license that keeps it free for teaching ("go with PolyForm Noncommercial, keep atiic MIT"), and a way to install it and use it offline.

## Where I was wrong

- **A diagram that explained nothing.** A drawing of a "zone 2" on one page meant something to me and nothing to its reader: "I do not know what zone2 supposed to mean." It went.
- **Dark mode, twice.** The drawings kept their white backgrounds in dark mode, and my first fix, a half-transparent background, made the labels hard to read. The drawings now keep their light background, only slightly dimmed in dark mode, so the labels stay readable.
- **Too much at once.** Pages tended to grow: every view I could think of, every caption complete. The useful ones survived the bench; several didn't, and the site is better for it.

This site is built in public. The source is on GitHub, every change is there as it happens, and this blog tells the stories behind them: the sensors, the ideas, the questions. Building in public means anyone can see all of it. The licenses say what they may do with what they see.

There are three now: one for the drivers, one for the site's code, one for its content. They came out of a few short questions, and each answer says something about building in the open.

## Day one: MIT for everything

When atiic was reloaded on 4 November, the question came up straight away, and the answer was quick (i2c.ninja followed it):

> go with MIT

Open drivers, an open site, anyone free to do anything. A day later, with the first device pages live and the site starting to look like something a classroom could use, Weihong thought again: education should stay free, but a company building the site into its own product is a different case. The answer:

> go with PolyForm Noncommercial, keep atiic MIT

Two licenses, then, for two different jobs.

## atiic: MIT, on purpose

atiic is [the datasheet in code](/blog/reloaded): drivers loyal to their chips, not to any one microcontroller or framework. That only works if they can go anywhere: into a hobby project, a school's lab, a company's test rig. So atiic stays MIT, the most permissive license there is.

That includes the hardest piece of code in the whole stack: the CH341 adapter that lets a browser talk I2C over USB. It lives in atiic, it runs in Node as well as in the browser, and its board tests run without any browser at all. Weihong's condition for leaving it open was simple:

> as long as atiic being self-sufficient and testable in nodejs. it woud be fine.

## The site's code: noncommercial

i2c.ninja's own code is the layer on top: the pages, the live charts, the glass box, the gauges, the bit of glue that opens the dongle from a browser. That is under the [PolyForm Noncommercial](https://polyformproject.org/licenses/noncommercial/1.0.0) license: free for study, hobbies, research, schools and universities; commercial use by agreement. The first releases, up to 1.0.22, stay MIT for anyone who took a copy then.

## Code, or content?

Today the question came back with a twist:

> I think this one could be a topic of a new blog too, discuss about license choice, maybe I should reconsider the cc license.

Creative Commons licenses are made for writing and pictures, and Creative Commons itself advises against them for software. So the real question was where the line falls. Weihong asked it plainly:

> regarding the visuals, are they code or content?

The answer split them in two. The drawings (wiring guides, explainers, the comics on this blog) are pictures: drawn once, looked at, reused. That is content. The live views (charts, the attitude gauges, the glass box) are programs drawing from a sensor's readings. That is code. And the words on the pages are content, even where they sit inside code files.

So the content now has its own license: [CC BY-NC-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/). Attribution, noncommercial, share-alike: use it, adapt it, credit it, keep your version under the same terms.

## What it means, in practice

| You are | You may |
|---|---|
| a teacher | put the drawings and explanations on your slides and handouts, translate them, adapt them for your class, with credit |
| a student or a hobbyist | use the site, read the code, change it for yourself |
| a maker building a product | use atiic's drivers freely (MIT); for the site's code or content, ask |
| a company | write to support@meshmass.com for a commercial license |

Three licenses, one purpose: to build in public and keep it fair. Everything stays open to look at and learn from; the drivers go anywhere; teachers can take the drawings to class; and a business built on the work starts with a conversation.

A sensor lesson usually begins a long way from the sensor. Before the first number there's a microcontroller board to buy, an IDE to install, a driver for its USB port, a library for the chip, a language to learn, and a sketch that compiles on the third try. By then the lesson has been about a tech stack for an hour, and the accelerometer hasn't said a word.

On 11 November, writing the Get started page's last step, "What it gives you", Weihong put the other way in plainly:

> another highlight of i2c.ninja is that. it helps students to focus on the physcis part, like science. and a simple usb-to-i2c bridge, they can get readings and visuals. There is no need to dive into 'arduino', 'raspberry pi', and learn another tech stack to make it work. well, they may come to that part later. but that would not be what we offer here.

That's now a card on the home page, above all the others: **The science first, programming optional.**

## Four wires and a browser

What's left, once the tech stack is gone, is short. A sensor module, four wires to a USB-to-I²C bridge, and a Chromium browser. The page opens the bridge over WebUSB, finds the sensor by its ID and shows its readings live: numbers, charts, a needle, a horizon. No board to program, no driver, no app. Once visited, the site even works offline.

A student puts an accelerometer flat on the desk and it reads 1 g, pointing up. That's the first surprise, and it's physics, not setup: why up, when gravity pulls down? The [acceleration page](/physics/acceleration) answers it: the chip doesn't feel gravity, it feels what holds it. The lesson has started, and nobody has typed a line of code.

## Physics and maths, all the way down

"The science first" needed one correction early on. A first draft of the card said the lesson goes to the physics; Weihong added the other half:

> the lessons goes to physics and maths.

and then what that means, step by step:

> what is measured, why and how. what the number tell and how apply it to real world.

That's a fair outline of every page on the site. [Physics](/physics) explains what each sensor measures and how a chip manages it: a tiny mass on springs, a mass kept shaking and pushed aside as it turns, a thin layer between a capacitor's plates taking up water vapour. The [Algorithms](/algorithms) pages take the numbers further, into what no single sensor gives: an attitude, steady as gravity and quick as the gyro, with every formula shown next to its live values. The maths isn't decoration on the readings. It's how the readings become an answer.

## Optional, not later

The card's title went through three versions in an afternoon. "No programming" sounded like a door shut on anyone who likes code. Weihong offered a few ways out:

> we can say programming later, or optional, even 'ai would take care of the programming part'.

"Later" would suggest programming is the next step for everyone, and for many students it simply isn't. "AI would take care of it" may well turn out true, but it's a forecast, and a home page should promise what the site does today. "Optional" says exactly that: nobody needs to program to learn here, and anyone who wants to can.

For those who do, the site has something to give as well. Every page runs on [atiic](https://github.com/aguegu/atiic), open drivers under MIT, field-tested on these pages with real modules. In Weihong's words, it "could be good reference if they would just use it or write their own driver". And before a sensor goes into anyone's project, a minute on its device page shows whether it answers and whether its readings are sane, which beats discovering a dead module three layers deep in someone else's code.

## Play, experiment, report

Taking the stack away leaves time, and the time goes to experiments: the things to try on each page, then a student's own questions. Since 1.8.0 every live page can record: one button beside Stop, and every reading goes into a plain CSV file, raw, with the time of each row and a column for each axis. The charts are the student's to make, in whatever tool they like. Weihong's verdict:

> That would be great for a A+ home assignment I think.

For teachers the same pages work at the front of the room. Every live chart goes full screen with one click, ready for the projector. The drawings and explanations are free to put on slides and handouts, with credit, a paid course included.

## What this site isn't

i2c.ninja doesn't teach microcontrollers. That's a fine subject with plenty of good courses, and a student may well come to it later. Here the sensor and its physics come first, and the code stays out of the way until someone wants it.
